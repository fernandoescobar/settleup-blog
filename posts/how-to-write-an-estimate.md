An estimate is often the first piece of paper a customer sees from you. It sets the tone for the whole job. A good one makes it easy to say yes. A vague one invites questions, delays and the occasional argument later. Here is how to write a job estimate that is clear, fair and easy to approve.

## What an estimate is

An estimate (also called a quote) is a document that tells a customer what you expect a job to cost and what it includes. It comes before the work starts. Once the customer agrees, you do the job, and then you send an invoice.

Keep one thing in mind: the customer is deciding whether they trust you. Clarity is what earns that trust.

## 1. Start with the basics

At the top, include:

- Your business name and contact details
- The customer's name and the job address, if relevant
- The word "Estimate" and an estimate number
- The date you wrote it

A number lets you both refer to it later. Keep them in order, like invoice numbers.

## 2. Describe the scope in plain words

The scope is what you will actually do. This is the most important part, and the place where most misunderstandings begin. Write it so a stranger could read it and picture the job.

Compare:

- Vague: "Garden work, $500"
- Clear: "Remove old planting from the front bed, add two bags of topsoil, plant customer-supplied shrubs, and haul away debris. One visit."

The second version tells the customer what they get and tells you what you agreed to do.

## 3. Break it into line items

List each part of the job on its own line with a price. Depending on your work, a line might be:

- Hours at an hourly rate
- A flat fee for a defined task
- Materials, listed separately
- Travel or setup, if you charge for it

Line items help customers see where the money goes. They also make it easier to adjust: if the customer wants to drop one item, you change one line, not the entire quote.

## 4. Say what is not included

This section saves more friction than any other. A short list of exclusions sets boundaries before they are tested. Examples:

- "Does not include removal of large stumps."
- "Photos edited: up to 20. Additional edits quoted separately."
- "Customer to provide access to the property."

If something might reasonably be assumed to be included but is not, write it down.

## 5. State your assumptions

Estimates rest on guesses about the job. Say what you assumed, for example, "Based on a one-bedroom apartment in normal condition." If the reality differs, you have grounds to discuss an adjustment, and the customer already knows that.

## 6. Add terms and an expiry date

Prices change, and your schedule fills up. Include:

- **An expiry date.** For example, "This estimate is valid for 30 days." Choose a period that suits your work.
- **Payment terms.** When you will invoice, whether you need a deposit, and when payment is due.
- **How changes work.** For instance, "Changes to the scope may change the price. I will confirm any changes in writing first."

Rules about contracts and deposits can differ by state, so if you are unsure, check your state's official resources or ask an attorney.

## 7. Show the total clearly

Put the total at the bottom, where people look for it, with any taxes, discounts or deposit amounts shown on separate lines. If you are not sure whether sales tax applies to your work, check with your state's official tax resources or a tax professional.

## 8. Make approval easy

The simplest estimate is one the customer can say yes to in a single step. That can be a reply email saying "approved," a signature on the page, or a signature on your phone when you are on site. Tell them how to accept. If you have to chase for a yes, the process is too hard.

## 9. Read it as the customer

Before you send it, read your estimate as if you knew nothing about the job. Ask:

- Do I know what I am getting?
- Do I know what it costs?
- Do I know what happens next?
- Is anything surprising?

If the answer to the last question is yes, fix it before sending.

## 10. Follow up once

If you have not heard back in a few days, send a short, friendly check-in. People are busy, and silence usually means "later," not "no."
