If you are new to billing customers, "invoice" and "receipt" can sound like two names for the same thing. They are not. The difference between an invoice and a receipt comes down to one question: has the customer paid yet?

## The short answer

- An **invoice** asks for payment. You send it before the customer has paid.
- A **receipt** confirms payment. You send it after the customer has paid.

An invoice says, "Here is what you owe." A receipt says, "Thank you, this has been paid."

## What an invoice is for

An invoice is a request for payment. It tells the customer what work was done, what it costs and how and when to pay. It usually includes:

- Your business name and contact details
- The customer's name
- An invoice number and issue date
- A due date or payment terms
- An itemized list of the work or products
- The total amount owed
- How to pay

The invoice is the working document. The customer uses it to pay you, and you use it to keep track of who owes you what. ## What a receipt is for

A receipt is proof of payment. It tells the customer, and your own records, that money changed hands. A receipt typically shows:

- Your business name and contact details
- The customer's name
- The date the payment was received
- What the payment was for, ideally with a reference to the invoice number
- The amount paid
- How it was paid (cash, check, bank transfer, card)

A receipt does not ask for anything. It closes the loop. The free [receipt template](https://settleupapp.net/templates/receipt-template) gives you a layout to start from.

## When to send each

**Send an invoice** when the work is done, or at the stage you agreed on, such as a deposit or a milestone. Send it promptly while the job is fresh in the customer's mind.

**Send a receipt** when payment arrives. If someone pays you in cash at the door, hand over a receipt on the spot. If a customer pays by bank transfer a week after the invoice, send the receipt when you see the money.

Some customers will not ask for one, but it is good practice to offer. It saves arguments later about whether something was paid.

## Can one document do both?

Often, yes. Many people mark an invoice as "Paid" and add the payment date and method. That turns the same document into a record of payment. The important thing is that it is clear to the customer and to you that the money has been received. If you do this, keep the original invoice number on it so the paperwork stays consistent.

Some businesses prefer separate documents because it keeps the history tidy: one document for the request, another for the confirmation. Either approach is fine. Choose one and stick with it.

## Why keep both

Together, invoices and receipts tell the full story of a job: what you asked for and what you received. Good records help you monitor your business, track expenses and support items on your tax return, according to the IRS, which also says you can choose any recordkeeping system that clearly shows your income and expenses. See its [recordkeeping page](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/recordkeeping). For questions about what you need to keep for your own tax situation, ask a tax professional.

## Where estimates fit in

There is a third document in the chain. An **estimate** (or quote) comes before the work and says what you expect it to cost. The usual order is:

1. Estimate: before the work
2. Invoice: after the work
3. Receipt: after payment

## A simple example

A tutor agrees to ten lessons. She sends an invoice for the ten lessons at the agreed rate, due in 14 days. The parent pays by bank transfer on day nine. The tutor then sends a receipt showing the amount, the payment date and the invoice number it covers. The invoice asked. The receipt confirmed.

## Common mix-ups

- **Sending a receipt before payment.** If the money has not arrived, send an invoice, not a receipt.
- **No record of what a payment covered.** Always reference the invoice number on the receipt.
- **Treating "paid" as obvious.** Say it in writing, with the date.
